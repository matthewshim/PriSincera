Over more than twenty years of building global services across a range of domains, I have witnessed failure more times than I can count. I watched a much-anticipated product get ignored by the market. I watched a carefully built redesign come back as user anger. I watched a conviction-filled plan collapse in front of the data. What was interesting, though, was what came next. Faced with failures of the same size, some teams fell apart, some teams held, and — very rarely — some teams came out stronger than they had been before. Today I want to write about that third kind of team.

## Recovery and learning sit on different levels

We often talk about resilience: the strength to take a hit without breaking and return to where you were. It is a valuable thing, of course. But look closely and resilience is the language of *restoration*. If the place you return to is the state you were in before the failure, that team is quietly preparing to meet the same failure again.

Treating failure as a teacher is a different order of thing. It is not returning to the original state but *moving* to a better one than before the failure. If the former is restoration, the latter is learning. And when the two combine, you get a capability one level above plain resilience.

The concept that captures this most precisely is Nassim Taleb's *antifragile*. The fragile breaks under shock. The robust withstands it. But the antifragile grows stronger the more shock it takes. When Jensen Huang told young people he wished upon them ample doses of pain and suffering, I understand him to have been pointing at exactly this. Not an instruction to endure pain, but the insight that pain is the raw material of greatness. Add learning-from-failure to resilience and you get the ability to convert shock into an input for growth.

## AI is increasing the absolute volume of failure

Why does this capability matter precisely now? I think the reason is clear: AI is collapsing the cost of execution.

When the cost of building falls, the number of attempts rises. An experiment that once required committing a whole quarter can now be run several times in a single week. But as attempts rise, so does the absolute volume of failure. An organization that used to try ten things and now tries a hundred will fail ten times as often. This is not an avoidable side effect; it is a structural consequence.

Once you see that, you can see where the axis of competition moves. In an era when attempts were scarce, *the ability not to fail* was the competitive edge. In an era when attempts are cheap, *learning per failure* is the competitive edge. If out of the same hundred failures one organization extracts a hundred lessons while another accumulates only scar tissue, the gap between them compounds within the same span of time. The board has been reset: the winner is not the organization that avoids failure, but the one that digests failure fastest.

## Failure is verified in the field, every day

Anyone who has operated a living service knows it. Launch is not the end but the beginning, every week is an experiment, and every update is a hypothesis. And on a global stage, failure does not arrive wearing one face. The same product is loved in one market and ignored in another. The same policy works in one region and enrages users in another.

At that moment an organization has only two choices. Treat failure as an *incident* and look for who is responsible, or treat failure as *data* and look for the lesson. In organizations that choose the former, failure gets hidden — and hidden failure always repeats. In organizations that choose the latter, failure is recorded and shared, and it changes the judgment applied to the next market, the next redesign, the next product. Looking back over twenty years, the difference between those two kinds of organization was not a difference in talent. It was a difference in how they handled failure.

## Information manages failure — not strictness

There is something here that must not be misread. Saying we should treat failure as data is not saying we should become numb to it. But the trap on the opposite side is far more common: organizations that treat failure too strictly.

In an organization where failure is followed by blame, what people learn is not the lesson but concealment. Failures vanish from the reports, experiments shrink to a size small enough not to get anyone punished, and the organization looks flawless on the surface while it actually stops learning. In an era when the absolute volume of failure is bound to rise, assigning guilt to each individual failure is cutting the organization's own learning circuit.

The principle I have arrived at is this. What an organization should be strict about is not failure itself, but the failure to learn from it. Be generous with failure, and serious about the absence of learning. And even that seriousness should take the form of *adjustment*, not reprimand. When the same failure repeats, ask before you punish a person: was the lesson never extracted, did the extracted lesson never reach the person who needed it, or was there a structure that could not be changed even though everyone knew? The remedies for those three cases are entirely different, and none of the three is solved by rebuke.

So in the end, the heart of managing failure is the flow of information. Every failure surfacing, the lesson being recorded, and that record arriving in time in the hands of whoever makes the next decision. I would rather call this *a culture that improves failure through information* than *a culture that reduces failure through strictness*. The first makes failure hide and leaves the organization fragile; the second makes failure visible and makes the organization antifragile.

## Organizations that keep winning book failure as an asset

Translated into the language of management, I think you could put it this way: there are organizations that account for failure only as a loss, and organizations that also account for it as a learning asset. No such line item exists on a real financial statement, of course. But it certainly exists in the ledger of decisions.

The organizations I have watched win over the long run shared something. There, the rhythm of proving an experiment fast or killing it fast and moving to the next hypothesis was the organization's basic tempo. A rejected hypothesis was treated not as a failure but as *a road erased from the map*, and the retrospective was a place for extraction rather than blame. And above all, the leader put their own failures on the table first. There is no faster way to show that sharing failure is safe.

At the individual level this capability is called *learning agility*: the ability to extract lessons quickly from novel situations and from the experience of failure, and to apply them next time. It is, in fact, one of the things people actually look at when assessing leadership potential. Lately you also hear AQ — adaptability quotient — alongside IQ and EQ. Whatever the name, they all point to the same place. If antifragility is the principle, learning agility is that principle translated into an individual competency. And an operating rhythm of proving fast and killing fast is the same principle implemented at the scale of an organization.

## In closing

If I had to define it in one line, I would write: *the ability to go beyond avoiding or enduring shock and failure, and instead convert them into raw material for learning, moving each time to a better state of judgment and readiness.* Now that AI is collapsing the cost of attempting things, this capability is becoming not a choice but a condition of survival — for individuals and for organizations alike.

Today, again, I am sitting in front of a failure. Yesterday's experiment rejected the hypothesis, and I have to build the next one. It is not a pleasant place to sit. But because I know this failure carries us toward better judgment than we had yesterday, I believe it is the most productive seat in the organization. Not merely not breaking, but making the shock that would break you into material. That, I think, is the most important muscle for anyone accountable for a service in this era.
