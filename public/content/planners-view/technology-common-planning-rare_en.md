Over more than twenty years of building services across a range of domains, I have watched the moment an organization's fate turns more than a few times. What is interesting is that the moment was rarely a failure of engineering, and rarely a failure of planning. Failure was almost always born in the gap between the two. Today I want to write down some thoughts about the two people standing at either end of that gap — the one who leads technology, and the one who leads planning.

## The scarcity of technology is collapsing

Only a decade or so ago, "can we actually build that?" was the central question in the executive meeting. Servers that hold under massive concurrency, infrastructure spanning global regions, an architecture that absorbs traffic swinging by the second. These were scarce capabilities held by a handful of organizations, and the technical leader accountable for them was a competitive advantage simply by existing.

And now? Cloud tore down the barrier to entry for infrastructure, open source shared the answer key for architecture, and AI is dramatically driving down the cost of writing code. The answer to "can we build it?" is now, most of the time, yes. This does not mean technology got easy. It means technology has levelled up across the board — and a capability that everyone has levelled up on is no longer a source of differentiation.

So some people say the age of the technical leader is ending. I see it exactly the other way around. The role is not fading; the question is changing. From "can we build it?" to "what do we build ourselves, what do we buy, and what do we abandon?" The more common technology becomes, the rarer technical *judgment* becomes. Ranking possibilities in an age when everything looks possible — that, I believe, is the essential value of whoever leads technology today.

But one thing becomes clear here. That judgment is always a judgment **toward a destination**. What to buy and what to abandon can only be answered once you have settled where you are going. When the destination is blurry, even the most precise technical judgment misses precisely.

## The bottleneck moved to "what should we build?"

When the cost of building falls, the bottleneck naturally moves one step upstream. What do we build? Who are we building it for? Why does it have to be now? Answering these questions is what planning is. I am convinced that the value of whoever leads planning will grow far larger than it is today, because when execution becomes cheap, the scarce resource is not the ability to execute but a sense of direction.

And cheap execution comes with a price. Being able to build fast means being able to build the wrong thing fast. The development period itself used to act as a buffer — while something took half a year to build, the market would sometimes hand you the answer first. That buffer is gone. The more AI raises our speed, the higher the cost we pay when the direction is wrong. So I believe the center of gravity that decides an organization's fate is moving from *how well we build* to *what we decide to build*.

Over the past four-odd years leading web services at a game company, I have met a domain where this proposition shows up with unusual clarity. The global games business is mixed martial arts for planning instinct. Even for the same game, every region loves it differently, pays differently, and gets angry at a different thing. In live service, launch is not the end but the beginning, so user voices, data, and business judgment collide every single day. Inside that complex system, deciding "what do we do next" is something no algorithm will do on your behalf. A deep understanding of users, an instinct for the market, and the courage to give up what must be given up. The work of planning keeps getting harder — and that is exactly why it keeps getting more precious.

## But you cannot fly on one wing

That is where the center of gravity of my thinking sits. But I want to be precise about one thing. Saying the center of gravity is moving toward planning does not for a moment mean engineering has become less important. That would be the opposite of what I learned in the field.

Planning without engineering stays a document. Engineering without planning stays a demo. I have watched excellent plans slip half a year against a wall of legacy architecture, and I have watched astonishing technology disappear quietly because it never connected to a real user problem. Neither case was one person's failure. Both were structural failures — two leaders climbing separate mountains with different maps in hand.

Conversely, when I recall the moments an organization shone brightest, the two of them were, without exception, at the same table. Planning says "users drop off right here," and engineering answers "if we pay down the debt at that point this quarter, our experiment velocity doubles." The moment the language of planning and the language of engineering move between each other without translation, a roadmap stops being a document and becomes momentum. In a global service, even the decision to add one localization or one payment method is in truth a joint product of planning judgment and technical judgment. The moment either side treats the other as "the department that receives requests," the organization's speed is cut in half.

## What organizations that keep winning have in common

Looking back, among the organizations I have watched, the ones that win over the long run shared something unmistakable. There, technology and planning were not a relationship of trading requests. They were partners writing the growth equation together.

In those organizations, the two leaders were looking at the same numbers. The moment engineering is evaluated on stability and planning on metrics, the two are structurally set up to collide; but the moment they jointly own a single goal of user value and business outcome, the old argument between paying down debt and shipping new features turns from politics into strategy.

In those organizations, the roadmap was a single sheet. Not a sequential process where planning draws and engineering reviews — from the very beginning, the two drew one roadmap together. The virtuous cycle where technical possibility widens planning's imagination, and planning's ambition sets the direction of technical investment, happened only on that one sheet.

And in those organizations, the two leaders clashed often. An organization without clashes was an organization where one of the two had gone silent. But the collision always happened on the same table, with data, aimed at a single conclusion. Disagreement being spent in the meeting room rather than in the hallway — it looks minor, but I believe it is the difference that decides an organization's lifespan.

## In closing

Now that AI is collapsing the cost of execution, I paradoxically feel that an age is coming in which human judgment is the most expensive thing of all. The judgment of what to build, and the judgment of how to build it. Of the two, the first has to stand first — because for an organization that has not decided what to build, how to build it is not yet a question you can ask.

So when people say the age of planning is coming, I now think they are right. But that age does not arrive by pushing engineering aside. It arrives standing on engineering, and alongside it.

Only when both wings beat in the same rhythm does a business gain altitude. Today, again, I spend the largest part of my time on keeping that rhythm — because I believe it is the most important work for anyone accountable for a service in this era.
