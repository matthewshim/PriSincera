We have arrived in an age where you can ask AI anything and a plausible answer comes back in seconds. To me — after more than twenty years of building global services across a range of domains — this change feels like a blessing and, at the same time, a new kind of danger. In an age when answers were scarce, the search for one forced us to stare at the problem for a long time. In an age when answers pour out, we hold an answer in our hands before we have even looked at the problem. And a plausible answer can be produced just as easily on top of a question that has already missed the essence.

So I believe the decisive ground of the AI era is shifting from *the ability to obtain answers* to *the ability to grasp the essence*. And at the center of that ability sits a startlingly simple word. The question "why?"

AI is strong at *what* and *how*. Tell it what to build and it will work out how to build it in an instant. But *why* still belongs to the person asking. Why is this a problem, why now, why this approach? An answer obtained without that question, however refined, is only speed without direction. The trouble is that asking "why" well is harder than it sounds. A child asks why dozens of times a day; an adult loses the question within a few years of organizational life. In an environment where pretending to know the answer looks safer than asking, *why* is a muscle that atrophies without training.

The people I have watched in the field who saw the essence clearly were, without exception, people who had trained that muscle for a long time. But their "why" did not point in one direction. They asked it of the problem, of convention, of their own understanding, of themselves, and of time. Today I want to write about those five directions of "why" — the things you can train in order to see the essence.

## First, asking "why" of the problem — training to rewrite the question

The most common route to missing the essence is not a wrong answer but a wrong question. Nothing is a more elaborate waste than answering the wrong question precisely. Hand a task to AI as given and it will solve that task without doubting it — which means solving the wrong question elegantly has only gotten faster. So the first training is to doubt the question before looking for the answer.

The method is simple: descend by asking "why" of the problem in front of you. Given the task "we need to reduce churn," instead of starting work, ask. Why do they leave? Why do they not feel a reason to stay? Why did we fail to create that reason? The classic practice of asking why about five times has survived for over half a century because it is the most reliable staircase from surface symptom down to structural cause. Each "why" peels back one layer of the problem, and only then can you choose which layer to fight on. Rewriting the problem in your own words is the check on that descent.

## Second, asking "why" of convention — training to take the obvious apart

The second is first-principles thinking. A great deal of what we believe to be essence is in fact convention. In the sentence "this industry has always done it this way," *always* is usually nothing more than a constraint from some moment in time that has fossilized. Once that constraint disappears, the convention survives and poses as essence.

The tool for breaking that fossil is also "why." Pick one familiar practice and ask. Why do we do it this way? Why did this practice come about? Is that reason still valid? If it is not, why is it still here? Someone who has made this a habit arrives at a different place than someone who thinks by analogy. Analogy starts from "what are others doing," while "why" descends all the way to "what is the principle that does not change." And most of what AI pours out is, by nature, closer to analogy — it is produced inside the gravity of answers that already exist. Without training to ask "why" of convention, we mistake the pull of that gravity for the essence.

## Third, asking "why" of your understanding — training to define

The third is building precision in language. I tend to think that if you cannot define a concept in a single sentence, you do not yet know it. A definition is, in the end, an answer to "why is this different from that." If you cannot answer why resilience differs from antifragility, your strategy for handling failure gets blurry. If you cannot answer why growth differs from expansion, the direction of your investment gets blurry.

So the training is writing. Take the problem you are wrestling with and write it out in ten lines or fewer, without jargon. The point where you get stuck while writing is exactly where your understanding ends — and that point is precisely where the next "why" belongs. Between what you can say fluently and what you can write clearly runs a wide river, and the essence is on the far bank. This is also why having AI summarize something and writing the definition with your own hand are entirely different acts. The former lets you skip understanding; the latter sits you down face to face with "why."

## Fourth, asking "why" of yourself — training to kill your own hypothesis

The fourth is the muscle of disproof. People instinctively gather evidence that confirms their own hypothesis. AI amplifies that instinct. Because it tends to answer in a way that respects the asker's premises, asking with conviction tends to return an answer that reinforces the conviction.

What you need here is a "why" pointed in the opposite direction — a "why" aimed at yourself. Why do I believe this is right? Is the ground for that belief verified, or merely comfortable? If this hypothesis is wrong, why would it be wrong, and what signal should appear at that point? Even when putting the same question to AI, the person who asks "attack this plan and tell me why it would fail" rather than "organize the case for this plan" extracts an entirely different value from the same tool. A "why" asked of others produces insight; a "why" asked of yourself produces humility and precision. The essence usually reveals itself at the place where my own conviction collapses.

## Fifth, asking "why" of time — training to find what does not change

The last is training that stretches the time axis. The surest touchstone separating essence from fashion is time. The names of technologies change every few years, but the fact that people want to be recognized, want to be connected, and want to save time does not change. Fashion is only the form of expression laid on top of that.

Here "why" aims at persistence. Was this true ten years ago, and if so, why was it true? Will it be true ten years from now, and if so, what sustains it? Only when you can ask "why does it not change" as readily as "why does it change" does it become clear which of the changes in front of you is a shift in essence and which is surface noise. Now, when discourse about the AI era pours out faster by the day, is exactly when this touchstone is needed most.

## In the end, every training converges on "why"

Having written the five out, it becomes clearer. Rewriting the problem, taking convention apart, writing the definition, attacking the hypothesis, finding what does not change — all of it is, in the end, throwing one question in five different directions. Seeing the essence is not a separate talent; it is a repertoire of directions and depths for asking why.

And the fact that this is something you can train feels hopeful to me. Asking "why" is tedious, and it looks like it slows you down in the moment. All the more so in an age when the answer arrives in seconds. But looking back, among the people I have worked with, the ones who changed an organization's direction were always the ones who never stopped asking this tedious question. It was not that they answered faster than everyone else. It was that while everyone else ran off holding an answer, they paused half a beat and asked "why" one more time. In an age when execution has become cheap, that half beat is no longer a delay — it is the highest-return investment available.

## In closing

That said, this question is not sustained by individual will alone. Just as learning stops first in an organization that punishes failure, the question disappears first in an organization where the person asking "why" looks slow. For the training to continue, it has to be an organization that will wait out that half beat.

Today, again, I ask "why" before I take hold of an answer. The more the age lets AI build anything, the more the value grows of the person who knows why it should be built at all. Seeing the essence does not appear overnight. But that it can be trained — that much I can vouch for with my own experience.
